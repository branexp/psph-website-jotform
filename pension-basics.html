Pension Basics

A plain-language starting point for public school employees. Rules differ by state, pension system, and hire date, so use this as background and confirm details with your pension system.

  • Social Security after the WEP/GPO repeal

Vesting and service credit

Your pension is usually based on three things: your years of service credit, your final average salary, and a multiplier set by your plan. Service credit is earned for time worked; part-time work may earn partial credit.

Vesting is the point at which you have earned a right to a lifetime pension. Most public plans require several years of service (commonly 5 to 10, depending on your state and tier). If you leave before vesting, you can usually withdraw your own contributions, but not the employer-funded pension.

  • Find your tier: rules often differ based on when you were hired.
  • Check your annual statement for your current service credit.
  • Before leaving a job, find out what you would give up.

Social Security after the WEP/GPO repeal

The Social Security Fairness Act, signed in January 2025, repealed the Windfall Elimination Provision (WEP) and the Government Pension Offset (GPO). Those rules had reduced Social Security benefits for many people who also receive a pension from work not covered by Social Security.

The repeal applies to benefits payable for months after December 2023, and the Social Security Administration has paid retroactive amounts to affected people. If your benefit was reduced in the past, or you didn't apply because of WEP or GPO, it may be worth checking your record now.

  • Whether your school job pays into Social Security depends on your state and district.
  • Your Social Security statement at ssa.gov shows your earnings record.
  • Spousal and survivor benefits may also have changed under the repeal.

Buying service time

Many plans let you purchase service credit for time that wouldn't otherwise count, such as prior out-of-state teaching, military service, approved leaves, or service you previously withdrew. More service credit usually means a larger pension, and it can sometimes let you retire earlier.

The cost depends on your plan's formula and often goes up the longer you wait. Plans commonly accept a lump sum, payroll deductions, or a direct transfer from a 403(b) or 457(b) account.

  • Ask your pension system for a written cost quote.
  • Compare the cost with the added lifetime benefit.
  • Check purchase deadlines; some options expire.

403(b) and 457(b) basics

A 403(b) and a governmental 457(b) are voluntary savings plans offered by many school employers on top of your pension. Contributions are typically made by payroll deduction, either pre-tax or as Roth contributions where your plan offers them.

The two plans have separate IRS contribution limits, so you may be able to contribute to both. Money in a governmental 457(b) can generally be withdrawn after you leave your job without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty that applies to most retirement accounts before age 59½.

  • Check which plans your employer offers.
  • Review the fees and investment options in your plan.
  • Remember these accounts are separate from your pension.

Choosing when to retire

Most plans set a normal retirement age or a combination of age and service (for example, a "rule of 80"). Retiring before you meet it usually means a permanently reduced benefit, while each additional year of service can raise it.

When you retire you will typically choose a payout option, such as a benefit for your lifetime only or a smaller benefit that continues to a survivor. In most systems this choice can't be changed after payments begin.

  • Request benefit estimates for more than one retirement date.
  • Understand your survivor options before you choose.
  • Plan for health insurance between retirement and Medicare.
